    Peptide Therapy Explained: BPC-157, CJC-1295, NAD+ and What the Evidence Says

    Oakwell MD care team · August 31, 2026 · 6 min read

    Peptides are short chains of amino acids, the same building blocks that make up proteins. Insulin is a peptide, and so is the GLP-1 hormone your gut releases after meals. In wellness clinics, the word usually refers to a smaller group of injectable or nasal compounds marketed for recovery or aging. Evidence for them varies widely. Some are FDA-approved for narrow uses, and many are not approved for anything.

    Approval status matters for a practical reason. An FDA-approved drug has passed controlled trials for a specific use, and its manufacturing is inspected. A compounded peptide skips that process. That does not make it useless, though it leaves you with less proof and fewer quality checks than an approved product carries.

    BPC-157

    BPC-157 is a lab-made fragment of a protein found in stomach juice. Rodent research hints at faster tendon and gut healing, yet controlled human trials are close to nonexistent. It is not FDA-approved. The FDA has listed it among bulk substances that raise significant safety concerns for compounding, citing limited human safety data. Rules here have shifted in recent years, so ask your provider about its current status.

    People usually take BPC-157 as a daily injection under the skin near the injured area, or as an oral capsule. Reported side effects in clinic settings include injection-site irritation and nausea, but nobody has collected this in a systematic way. There is also a theoretical concern. Because it promotes blood vessel growth in animal studies, some experts worry about use in people with a history of cancer.

    CJC-1295, ipamorelin and sermorelin

    These compounds prompt the pituitary gland to release more of your own growth hormone. Sermorelin was once FDA-approved for children with growth hormone deficiency and was later discontinued by its maker for business reasons, and it is now available only through compounding. CJC-1295 and ipamorelin have never been FDA-approved. Most of the fat loss and sleep claims rest on small, short studies. Side effects can include water retention and higher blood sugar, and flushing at the injection site is common.

    Growth hormone releasers can affect blood sugar and are usually avoided in people with active cancer. A provider will typically check IGF-1, a blood marker of growth hormone activity, before starting and again after a few months. Injections are often taken at bedtime. Most protocols run in cycles.

    Tesamorelin is the exception. Sold as Egrifta, it carries FDA approval for reducing abdominal fat in people with HIV-related lipodystrophy. Using it for general weight loss or aging in people without that condition is off-label, and insurance almost never covers that use. Expect a glucose check before you start and periodically after.

    NAD+

    NAD+ is a coenzyme every cell uses to turn food into energy, and levels drop with age. It is sold as IV infusions, shots or nasal sprays, mostly marketed for energy and healthy aging. Human data is limited. Oral precursors such as nicotinamide riboside raise blood NAD+ levels in studies, though clear real-world benefits have not been proven. Fast IV infusions commonly cause flushing and chest tightness, so they are usually run slowly.

    NAD+ injections are usually given a few times a week at first, then less often. They are not FDA-approved for any condition, and most of the excitement comes from animal research on aging and cell energy rather than from human trials. Expect a mild flush.

    Questions to ask before starting a peptide

    • Is this product FDA-approved, and for what?
    • What human studies support the use I am interested in?
    • Which licensed pharmacy compounds it, and is it a 503A or 503B facility?
    • What side effects should I watch for, and what labs will you check?
    • How long will I take it, and how will we know if it works?

    Skip anything sold online as research use only. They are not intended for people, and nobody verifies what is actually in the vial. Bremelanotide, sold as Vyleesi, is an FDA-approved peptide for low sexual desire in premenopausal women, which shows that peptides can earn approval when the trials are done.
